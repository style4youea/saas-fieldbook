Country Selector vs Geo Redirect: Help International Shoppers Reach the Right Store

A shopper's location is a useful clue. It does not always tell you where they want to shop, which language they read or where an order should be delivered.

A customer opens a product link while travelling abroad. Your website detects the country and sends them to another storefront—where the product is missing. They go back, and the same redirect happens again.

The store tried to be helpful. The rule ignored the customer's intent. International routing works better when you decide what should happen in these awkward cases before installing a tool.

Our starting recommendation

For most small stores with separate regional sites, start with a visible country selector and an optional suggestion to switch. Preserve the visitor's ability to stay. Consider automatic routing only when you have a clear reason, reliable destination mappings and a tested escape route.

This is our editorial recommendation, not a measured conversion result.

1. Separate country, language and delivery destination

A visitor in Germany may prefer English and be buying a gift for delivery in France. Treating those as one setting can create the wrong catalog, an unexpected currency or a misleading shipping promise.

Before implementing location rules, write down what each storefront controls. Does it change language only? Does it change inventory, prices, delivery coverage or the business receiving the order? The answer determines how prominently you must explain a switch.

SettingQuestion it answersUseful control
LanguageWhich language does the visitor want to read?A clear language choice that remains accessible.
Shopping marketWhich regional catalog and commercial offer apply?A market selector with understandable country names.
Delivery countryWhere must the order go?Explicit address and shipping checks in the buying journey.
Detected locationWhere does the current connection appear to originate?A suggestion that the visitor can correct.

Do not let a location banner promise a delivery price your checkout cannot honor. A change to displayed text is not evidence that your tax, catalog or shipping system has also changed.

2. Choose how much control the rule should have

A manual selector is a good baseline: it gives visitors a route without making assumptions. A suggested switch adds a prompt when a regional option appears relevant. An automatic redirect makes the decision immediately and therefore needs the most careful exception handling.

For an illustrative store with UK and EU storefronts, a helpful message could be: “Shopping for delivery in the EU? Visit our EU store, or stay on this site.” Describe the shopping task rather than presenting detected location as certainty.

Keep “stay” easy to find, avoid a forced countdown and provide a persistent selector after the banner closes. A remembered choice should remain changeable. These are design recommendations to test against your own audience.

3. Keep regional pages accessible to search engines

Google advises against automatically redirecting people between language versions based on an assumed language, and recommends links that let visitors choose. It also recommends separate URLs for language versions and appropriate annotations. See Google's guidance for multilingual and multi-regional sites.

Google separately explains that location-adaptive pages may not have all variants crawled, indexed or ranked: Googlebot's usual IP locations and lack of an Accept-Language header can affect what it sees. See its locale-adaptive crawling documentation.

The practical implication is to treat a geo tool as one part of a regional site architecture. Do not assume adding a redirect or an hreflang tag automatically fixes every discovery problem. Keep useful regional URLs reachable through ordinary navigation and check the pages you actually want indexed.

4. Map destinations before activating rules

Start with a short mapping sheet: source page, destination page, market, product availability and fallback. A visitor following a specific product link should not be dropped onto an unrelated homepage without explanation.

When an equivalent product does not exist, decide what is useful: stay on the original page, show a clear availability message or offer a relevant category. A fallback is a business decision. Do not let an unreviewed rule decide it accidentally.

Also test links containing campaign parameters and fragments, cart or account pages, and existing redirect rules. Two systems trying to “correct” a visitor's country can create loops. Establish which system owns routing and how the rule can be disabled.

Where Geo Targetly fits

Geo Targetly documents location-based rules and both automatic and optional popup redirects. Its Geo Redirect documentation describes allowing visitors to stay and remembering their selection. Those features make it a candidate for the suggested-switch approach; they do not establish that your implementation is correct. See the official Geo Redirect documentation.

The vendor also offers a Geo Browse tool that produces screenshots from selected countries through proxies. It can help inspect what is displayed. Treat that as a visual check alongside, rather than a replacement for, interactive journey testing.

Before buying, check whether your commerce platform already provides the market selection you need. A separate tool is most useful when it fills a specific gap. Evaluate current traffic allowances, supported domains, the required product and the full billing commitment on the pricing page.

Count usage the way the vendor counts it

Geo Targetly's pricing FAQ says ordinary page visits consume quota for each product used, including visits that are not geo-targeted; Geo Links uses link clicks instead. It lists all-product access and unlimited domains, so quota can matter more than the domain count. Check the quota rules before choosing a plan.

In a simplified planning example, 10,000 visits with two products running on each visit could consume 20,000 units rather than 10,000. This is illustrative arithmetic, not measured usage. Verify actual consumption during the trial and include non-targeted traffic in your estimate.

Measure whether the rule helped

Define success before launch. Useful measures include how often visitors accept or dismiss the suggestion, whether they reach the intended product and whether support receives fewer wrong-market questions. Compare similar traffic where possible. A change in total revenue alone cannot isolate the effect of routing from seasonality, campaigns or catalog changes.

Keep an explicit rollback decision: if users become trapped, lose their journey or reach unavailable products, disable or narrow the rule and investigate. A modest prompt that visitors understand is more useful than a clever rule they cannot escape.
